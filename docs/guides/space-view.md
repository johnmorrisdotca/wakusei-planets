`@johnmorrisdotca/wakusei-planets/space/element` registers `<wakusei-space>`: the Earth, the Moon, the Sun, the seven other planets, Pluto, the eight other dwarf planets and eight minor bodies (Bennu, Eros, Vesta, Pallas, Chiron, Pholus, Halley's comet and Arrokoth) in their places for a date and time, with every moon the package knows round its planet, drawn with three.js through the page's one shared WebGL context. The Moon's phase and the Earth's sunlit side come out of the light, not out of a table. Zoom from the Earth out to the whole solar system and back, and run time forwards or backwards.

```html
<!doctype html>
<html lang="en">
  <head>
    <meta charset="utf-8" />
    <meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1" />
    <title>The Earth, the Moon and the planets on 2 November 2026</title>
    <script type="module" src="https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/@johnmorrisdotca/wakusei-planets/space/element/+esm"></script>
  </head>
  <body>
    <wakusei-space time="2026-11-02T14:00Z" speed="0" look="realistic" style="width: 420px"></wakusei-space>
  </body>
</html>
```

The element needs `three` (an optional peer). The pictures come from jsDelivr, or from the folder named by `texture-base`. In this view every body that has a surface picture is drawn from it in both looks (`look="realistic"` and `look="painted"`), the Moon and Mars too, and the finer map tiles come to them when the camera is near; a body with no picture (Phobos, Deimos, Uranus's moons) is shaded from its numbers. Your own three.js scene gets the same with `mapped: true` in the painter's context. Everything is drawn by the package's own painter ([in your own three.js scene](in-your-own-scene.md)), so the looks are the same as in `<wakusei-body>`.

## Where things are

Positions come from this package's own models and nothing else: the Sun and the planets from the VSOP87B series, Pluto from a table fitted to JPL Horizons, the Moon from ELP2000. They are geometric places in J2000 equatorial axes. The space view does not apply aberration or nutation, which would move a body by about 20 arc-seconds: far less than a pixel at this scale. The pure functions are in `@johnmorrisdotca/wakusei-planets/space` and import on a server:

```ts
import { frameAt, placeAround, placeSun } from "@johnmorrisdotca/wakusei-planets/space";

const frame = frameAt(Date.UTC(2026, 10, 2, 14));
const sun = placeSun("compressed", frame.sun);
const [x, y, z] = placeAround("compressed", sun, [frame.planets.mars[0] - frame.sun[0], frame.planets.mars[1] - frame.sun[1], frame.planets.mars[2] - frame.sun[2]]);
console.log(Math.hypot(x - sun[0], y - sun[1], z - sun[2]).toFixed(0));
```

`frameAt(ms)` is the Sun, the Moon and the planets in astronomical units from the Earth's centre. Against astronomy-engine (a dev-only test oracle) the Sun and the Moon agree to a hundredth of a degree and the planets to a few hundredths. `createTrack(stepMs)` answers for any moment from two real ones and interpolates, so a draw at a day a second costs about two lookups a second, not one a frame.

## Two scales

Space is mostly empty, so a picture at true scale is mostly black, with the bodies as specks. The element has both and says which with `scale`; it opens at true distances (`true`, the default):

| `scale` | Distances | Sizes |
|---|---|---|
| `true` (default) | Real distances and sizes, the Earth's equatorial radius for a unit | real: the planets are specks and the Sun is a dot with a glow. A body whose disc is under 4 CSS pixels across its radius is marked with a ring round its place as well as its name, so no planet is lost |
| `compressed` | Every direction is real. The Moon is drawn 3.5 Earth radii away (it is 60); the Sun 70 (it is 23,500); each planet is drawn in its real direction from the Sun at 70 radii times its distance in astronomical units to the power 0.45, so the Earth's own orbit is the 70 and Neptune's about 320 (it is 30 times as far). The Sun, the bodies and their orbit lines all go through the one mapping (`placeBodies`, `orbitLine`), so every body, the Earth too, lies on its own orbit line. The moons of a planet are drawn nearer when their real distance would cross the next planet's orbit (`moonDistance`): the farthest at a third of the gap, the others in the order they are, and none nearer than 1.5 radii or inside the planet's rings, so a camera at a moon is never in the ring plane | the Sun 5, the Moon 0.273 (its true size beside the Earth: a quarter of it) and the planets 0.4 to 1.8 Earth radii (larger than life) |

The element always names the scale in use on its own view: "True distances" or "Distances squeezed to fit" (`lang="ja"` gives the Japanese, `space.scaleNames = { true: "..." }` a page's own words, and `::part(scale)` styles the line). A page that wants the old opening writes `scale="compressed"`. In the squeezed scale, `moon-size` (2 by default, also `space.moonSize`, from 1 to 8) draws moons that many times larger than life. 1 is the real size. True distances ignore it. Every moon of one planet gets the same factor, so they keep their real order of size. The factor is cut, and `stats.moonSize` says so (`clamped`), when a moon would be drawn larger than a quarter of its planet, into the planet or its rings, or across to the next moon's orbit. `stats.pictures.pending` is how many surface pictures are still being fetched; a sharper one draws one more frame when it arrives, so a test of a view at rest waits for it to be 0. Picking a moon, the name drawn on it once it is 24 pixels in radius, and whether one body hides another, all use this drawn size. `space.placeOf(id)` says where a body is on the screen now (its centre and radius in CSS pixels in the element).

## Time

`time` is an ISO date (`2026-11-02T14:00Z`) or `now`; `speed` is game seconds a second: `1` real time, `0` held still, `3600` an hour a second, `86400` a day a second, a negative number runs backwards. Setting `time` points the camera at the same place on the ground again. The Earth turns by its real rotation, the Moon shows its near side to the Earth, and each planet is lit from the Sun, so Venus has its crescent and the Moon its quarter.

`light="lit"` lights every body as if by daylight from the camera's side (the Sun is its own light), so a view of a night side is never a black frame. The bodies keep their true places either way.

## Nearby bodies and close approaches

The [space demo](https://johnmorrisdotca.github.io/wakusei-planets/space.html) has two tabs below the view. **Nearby** shows the five closest bodies to the body you choose at the displayed date. Distance in space is measured centre to centre in kilometres. The angle in Earth's sky is measured in degrees from Earth's centre.

**Next close approaches** shows up to five events in the next 90 days, when two planets, or the Sun and a planet, come within five degrees in Earth's sky. Choose a result to highlight the two bodies in the space view. Choosing a dated event also sets the demo clock to that moment and pauses it.

A page can set `space.highlightedBodies` to emphasize the names, rings and edge arrows of particular bodies. Set it to an empty array to return to the view's ordinary emphasis. The `/nearest` entry supplies the calculations. [Finding nearby bodies and close approaches](nearest.md)

## Zoom, looking at a body, naming

The element carries the drag, the pinch, Ctrl and the wheel, and the keys itself ([zoom and pan](zoom-and-pan.md)), from a millionth (the camera far enough out to see the whole solar system) to 3.6x. The camera sits `4.1 / zoom` radii of the body it looks at from that body: zoom 1 shows the whole body with a margin, about 80% of the view's shorter side across, in any shape of view, and the ceiling, 3.6, is where the finest map tiles run out of detail; the camera is held 1.05 radii from the surface, so past about 3.9 nothing moves. At 3.6 the surface is as sharp as the finest map tiles go (the Earth, and Mars and the Moon in the `painted` look, have them: [close zoom](close-zoom.md)), and they are fetched only when the camera is that near, for the part in view. Put the family's `<kazoku-view-controls for="space">` beside it for buttons and it reads the same range; `zoom-min` and `zoom-max` on either one change it, and `controls="none"` takes the element's own hands off. `space.zoomFor("moon" | "sun" | "inner" | "solar-system" | "earth" | "jupiter-moons")` gives the zoom that shows each part of the sky at the current scale and moment (the name in any case; a name that is no preset gives 1, the zoom that shows the body looked at whole), and `space.controller.set({ zoom })` (or, with the bar, `controls.controller.set({ zoom }, true, "jump")`) goes there. `space.setView(controls detail)` turns the camera round the body (`roll` turns the picture clockwise about the line of sight, and `panX` and `panY` move everything in it by exactly that many CSS pixels, right and down positive, at any depth): it is fixed to the stars, so the Earth turns under it as time runs.

`space.body = "jupiter"` points the camera at a body: any body of the view in any case, in English or Japanese (`earth`, `sun`, `moon`, `mercury` to `neptune`, `pluto`, and the moons of the planets such as `titan`); a name that is no body changes nothing. The name beside each body is a button (Tab, Enter). A press on a body that is not a drag fires `wakusei-body-click` (`wakusei-body-focus` for the keyboard) with `{ id, name, kind, host, radiusKm, distanceKm }`; `space.bodyInfo(id)` gives the same. `space.bodyNames = { mars: "火星" }` and `lang="ja"` change the words.

`space.pathOf(id, from, to, options)` says where a body goes over a stretch of time, on the screen: `steps` + 1 points (96 by default, from 1 to 512) from `from` to `to`, each `{ ms, x, y, behind }` in CSS pixels in the element as `placeOf` has them, worked out in the view's own scale (squeezed or true) and from the camera as it is now, so a page can draw a track that lies in the picture (the demo's Path row draws a body's days either side of the clock as dots). `behind` is true, with no place, where the body is behind the camera at that moment. `options.about` names the body the path is taken round: each place is the body's offset from `about` at that moment, put where `about` is now. A moon's default is its planet, so the Moon's fortnight is a ring round the Earth (about 190 degrees of it) whatever the Earth does meanwhile, and `about: null` is the line the body itself travels. The Sun, the planets, the Moon, the other major bodies and the moons of the planets are placed; any other name, or a call before the first draw, gives an empty list. It is worked out when asked from the last draw, nothing is stored, and a camera that turns or moves gives another path, so a page that draws one asks again as the view changes.

A body behind another (its place on the screen inside a nearer body's disc, which `placeOf(id).behind` names) has its name and ring faded out while the disc is over it, as if the disc hid them, and faded back as it comes out; the disc is the one drawn, at the radius `placeOf(id).r` gives, and the nearer body's own name always stays. The Oort cloud's note and the marks of the places on a body that is behind another go the same way. `covered="arrow"` (or `space.covered`) puts one small quiet arrow just outside the disc's edge instead, on the side nearest the hidden body, pointing in behind it, with the name of the hidden body that ranks highest (the order of the names below) beside it and the others counted (`Uranus +4`); the first press selects that body, and a second press goes to it. The arrow goes as the body comes out. `covered="name"` draws the names over the disc as versions to 0.57 did. A page that draws a label of its own over the view (a belt's name at its place on the belt) asks `space.coveredAt(point)` (a point in the scene's places, as `stats.camera` has them) for the body whose disc hides it, or `null`, and fades the label while there is one. `coveredBy`, `coverOf` and `coverArrows` from `/space` are the maths.

Names far from the middle of the view are quieter. The names, dots, rings and arrows of the bodies outside the system in the middle of the view (the body looked at, the body it goes round, and that body's moons) are drawn at half strength: they are still there to read, and what belongs to the body in the middle stands out. `far-opacity` (0 to 1; `space.farOpacity`, or the CSS property `--wakusei-far-opacity` on the element) sets how strong they are, and `1` draws every name alike. With the Sun in the middle, the Sun and the eight planets are at full strength and the moons and dwarf planets are far. Put the pointer on a body or its name, keep a finger on it, or reach its name with Tab, and its own system is drawn at full strength and the rest at the far strength (the Sun pointed at is a system of its own). `wakusei-body-hover` fires with the body (the same detail as `wakusei-body-click`) and its `system: { host, moons }`, and `wakusei-body-hover-end` (`{ id }`) when no body is under it any more, when the finger lifts, or when the element leaves the page: a page can show a second card for the body pointed at beside the one for the body in the middle. `space.hovered` reads it, and `systemOf(id)` from `/space` gives any body's system. None of it draws the picture again.

No two names overlap: when the boxes of two names would cross, the one that matters less is left out, and a name that is left out takes its ring with it. The order is the body looked at, the Sun, the planets, then the Moon; a tie goes to the body that is bigger on the screen. A moon gives way to its planet and a planet to the Sun, and zooming in brings the names back. `chooseLabels(boxes)` from `@johnmorrisdotca/wakusei-planets/space` is the same rule on plain boxes. A name stays inside the view: where it would run past the right edge it goes to the left of its dot instead (the dot then follows the text), and if it fits on neither side, or would pass the top or the bottom, it is held inside the edge; a body whose dot is itself outside the view keeps its name where it falls. `nameSpot(dot, size, frame)` from `/space` is that placing on plain numbers.

A planet's nearer moons appear round it once the planet is a few pixels across, at the places the package's moon models give (`zoomFor("jupiter-moons")`). The paths of the Earth and the planets round the Sun, and the Moon's path round the Earth, are drawn unless `orbits="off"`. Every line goes through the same mapping as the bodies, so each body lies on its own line in both scales; `stats.orbits` says which lines were drawn and how far each body is from its own, as a fraction of the line's radius (under 0.01 for a body on its line). The Moon's line is one sidereal month from the Moon's own place and is worked out again every few days of the clock; `moonOrbit(ms)` gives its points.

`body-label="painted"` writes each body's name on its own globe as well, turning with it, in the page's language: the looks, how many, where and the font are in [A name on the body](name-on-the-body.md). A body too small on the screen carries none. While a body carries its drawn name its floating name and dot fade out and leave their place to the others, and fade back when it is small again; `labels-always` keeps both.

**The names of the craft give way.** With `spacecraft` on, a craft's name is written beside its dot like a body's, but it gives way. A body's name (a planet's, a dwarf planet's or a moon's) always wins over any craft's. Two craft names that touch give way by rank: the craft chosen first, then a favourite (`space.favourites`, a list of craft ids), then the one nearer the body in view. A craft's name goes round its dot, to the left, above or below, before it is left out; the dot always stays. `space.keepOut` is a list of rectangles of the element's own box, `[{ x, y, w, h }]` in CSS pixels: the page's bar, its notices, its strip. No name is written under one, a body's included. `space.labelShort` (`{ juice: { en: "JUICE", ja: "ジュース" }, webb: "Webb" }`) writes a craft's label short, in the language shown; the full name stays the craft's name for a card, a press, an event and a screen reader. The package ships no short names, so the page hands the table it wants. The layout is `placeLabels` (and `chooseLabels`) from `/space`, which takes the boxes, their ranks, other places to try and the rectangles.

## Whole body and Close framing

`space.framingFor(id, "whole" | "close", options)` predicts a zoom for a body. It uses the beside camera for the moon it is currently beside. For another body it centres the normal camera on that body from the direction of the last draw, so a page can choose its arrival zoom before travelling. The diameter is measured against the shorter side of the picture. Whole body defaults to two thirds, including Saturn's ring envelope. Close defaults to 1.8 and measures the globe. Set `share` to use a different proportion or pass `zoom` to inspect an exact zoom.

The result gives the proposed `zoom`, the achieved `share`, `clamped` when the camera cannot reach the requested framing, and the predicted places of the body and companion in CSS pixels with pan reset. Pass `companion: "sun"` to see where the Sun would be. Beside a moon, its planet is the default companion. An off-screen companion in front of the camera can have coordinates beyond the edges. A companion behind the camera has a null place.

```ts no-run
import type { WakuseiSpace } from "@johnmorrisdotca/wakusei-planets/space/element";

const space = document.querySelector<WakuseiSpace>("wakusei-space")!;
const whole = space.framingFor(space.body, "whole", { companion: "sun" });
if (whole) space.controller?.set({ zoom: whole.zoom, panX: 0, panY: 0 });
```

The query reads the last settled draw and leaves the live view alone. It returns `null` before that draw, during a glide, for a region or along-orbit camera, or for an unknown or filtered-out body. `zoomFor("solar-system")` and the other preset calls are available as before.

## Bodies out of view

`space.sight()` says where each body is for the picture at the last draw, as a list of `{ id, kind, host, inView, behind, edge, x, y, along, angle }`. A body whose disc is on the picture is `inView`. For one that is not, `edge` is the side of the picture it lies beyond (`top`, `right`, `bottom` or `left`), `x` and `y` the point on that edge where a line from the middle of the view to the body crosses it, `along` the same point measured along the edge (from the left for the top and bottom, from the top for the sides), `angle` the way to it in degrees clockwise from the top of the picture, and `behind` is true while it is behind the camera. The list holds the Sun, the planets, the dwarf planets and the minor bodies, the moons of the body looked at and any other moon drawn, the asteroids of `/asteroids` with `kind: "small-body"` when the option `asteroids` is on, and it holds while riding along (without the body ridden on). `wakusei-sight` fires with `{ bodies }`, the same list, when a body comes into view or leaves it, goes behind the camera or comes out, changes edge, or moves 8 CSS pixels along its edge, at most four times a second while the view moves and once more when it settles; never at rest. The view works the list out only for a page that asks: the first `sight()` call or the first `wakusei-sight` listener asks for one draw, and the list is there from that draw on. The view draws no tabs itself: a page places its own on the edges its controls leave free, and a press on one calls `space.goTo(id)`. `sightOf` and `sightChanged` from `/space` are the maths.

Every entry also says how far off the line of sight the body is and how far away: `offAxis` is the angle in degrees between the camera's forward direction and the body (0 straight ahead, 180 straight behind), `distance` the distance from the camera in the view's units (Earth radii), and `distanceKm` the same in kilometres in the true scale and `null` in the compressed one, whose distances are drawn and not real. A page can rank its edge markers by nearness with them. An orbiter (with `orbiters` on) is an entry of its own, `kind: "orbiter"`, with the id `orbiter` takes (`lro`, `mro`); a press on its name sends `wakusei-orbiter-click` with `{ id, name }` as well as the `wakusei-body-click` it always sent.

`space.turnTo(id, { smooth, margin })` turns the camera where it stands until a body is in the picture, by the least turn, and returns whether it turned. A body is in the picture when its middle is inside the picture less `margin` of each side: `margin` is a fraction of the width for the left and right and of the height for the top and bottom, 0.15 by default, 0 to 0.45. A body already so, the body looked at, an id the view does not know and a view that is riding answer `false` and draw nothing. The camera does not move: the body becomes the one looked at, from where the camera stands, and the zoom is worked out from that distance (a body so far off that the zoom's lower limit would move the camera is not turned to). It glides the way a move does, or at once with `smooth: false` or for a reader who prefers reduced motion, and one draw ends it.

## Every body

The view offers every body the package knows, 70 of them: the Sun, the eight planets, the nine dwarf planets (Pluto, Ceres, Orcus, Haumea, Quaoar, Makemake, Gonggong, Eris, Sedna), the eight minor bodies (the asteroids Bennu and Eros and the Kuiper belt object Arrokoth, the asteroids Vesta and Pallas, the centaurs Chiron and Pholus, Halley's comet) and the 44 moons. `SPACE_BODIES` from `/space/element` lists the twenty-seven it places in the middle of its scene (the dwarf planets from their osculating elements, good for a few years either side of 2026; Vesta, Pallas, Chiron, Pholus and Halley from tables fitted to Horizons, within an arc-second, which the view loads itself; Bennu, Eros and Arrokoth from the Small-Body Database ellipse), and every moon is placed round its host, in its real orbit. Any of them can be at the middle of the view, gone to, hopped to and ridden (`space.ride("ceres")`, `space.ride("titan")`, `space.ride("vanth")`). A dwarf planet or minor body too small on the screen to see (under half a pixel across its radius: at true scale every asteroid and comet is) is not drawn, only named and ringed, and its picture is not made until it is looked at; a moon is drawn once its planet is a few pixels across. With `orbits` on the dwarf planets and the minor bodies have their lines too (Halley's is a long thin ellipse out to Neptune's distance, with its sharp turn round the Sun). `space.zoomFor("mars-moons")` (also `jupiter`, `saturn`, `uranus`, `neptune`, `pluto`, `orcus`, `haumea`, `eris`) gives the zoom that shows a planet with its nearer moons.

A body is drawn round wherever it is in the picture, in a window of any shape. A perspective camera draws a sphere away from the middle of the picture as an ellipse, long along the line to the middle, by 1 / cos of the angle off the line of sight; the view's field of view is 35 degrees from top to bottom, so in a wide window (2012 by 745) a planet at the picture's edge was a quarter as long again as it was wide, and the Sun, near and large, more. Each body is now drawn as the ellipsoid that projects to a circle: the sphere squeezed, across the line of sight to it, to `1 / sqrt(1 + tan²θ · d² / (d² − r²))` of its width for a body at an angle θ off the line of sight, at a distance d, with a radius r (done in the vertex shaders, `scene/round.ts`). Its colours, light, map, rings and name are the sphere's own, and its centre, its depth and everything the camera does (travel, arrival, framing, the Pair and Body frames) are unchanged. The radius a body has on the screen (`placeOf(id).r`, which a press is tested against and the finer map tiles are chosen by) is the drawn one, 1 / cos θ times that of the same body in the middle of the picture, and the marks laid over the Earth (`earth-places`) and over a body (`surface-names`, `places`) follow the squeezed surface. In a scene of your own the painter does the same when `update` is given the camera as `view` ([In your own three.js scene](in-your-own-scene.md)); `round: false` leaves the camera's own stretch.

The demo's picker is three clicks in a row: a kind (the Sun and planets, dwarf planets, small bodies, moons), for a moon the planet it goes round, then the body, with a search by name (English or Japanese) beside them and a "With its moons" button for a planet that has some. `space.bodyList()` is the data for any picker of your own.

`roadster` (and `space.roadster`) draws Starman and the Tesla Roadster as a small marked body on its orbit line with its name, from the launch in 2018 to the end of 2035, loading its path only when it is first shown: [Starman and the Tesla Roadster](roadster.md). `spacecraft` (and `space.spacecraft`) is off by default. `"all"` or `true` draws every deep-space craft whose record covers the date, as one set of dots with their names; a space-separated list draws those. A craft outside its record is not drawn, and `stats.spacecraft.status[id]` is `"outside"`. Each table loads the first time that craft is shown. `sight()` gives each drawn craft an entry with `kind: "spacecraft"` and the craft's own id. A press sends `wakusei-body-click` with that kind and does not move the camera.

`orbiters` (and `space.orbiters`) draws the spacecraft in orbit round the Moon and Mars as dots on their orbit lines with their names, once the body is big enough on the screen, loading their paths only when first shown: [The orbiters of the Moon and Mars](orbiters.md).

`asteroids` (and `space.asteroids`; off by default) places the 101 bodies of `/asteroids` in the view: each a point on its orbit on the date shown, drawn in the one instanced draw with the belts' points, with the nearest few (at most 12, within about an astronomical unit of the camera in the true scale) named in the page's language. A name is a button: a press sends `wakusei-body-click` with `{ id, name, kind: "small-body" }` and `wakusei-asteroid-click` with `{ id, name }`, and nothing else happens (going to an asteroid is a `goTo` or a `ride`, below). Each is an entry of `sight()` with `kind: "small-body"` (Vesta and Pallas keep `asteroid`, the kind the package's own bodies have). Two of them, `europa` and `dione`, have the id of a moon: with the option on the moon is still the moon, and the asteroid is the entry of that id with `kind: "small-body"`. The records are loaded when the option is first turned on, in a file of their own that a page that never turns it on does not fetch, and with it off nothing is worked out or drawn for them. The positions are two-body, good near each body's epoch (`ASTEROID_ACCURACY` of `/asteroids` says how good). Measured in Chromium drawing in software (24 draws each, the time stepped by the test, 2026-11-17): with the option on a draw gives the graphics card one more call (14 to 15) and holds six more GPU objects (105 to 111), and turned off again it is back to 14 and 105, frame for frame; a frame took a median of 66 ms and 89 ms at the 95th percentile with it off and 74 ms and 106 ms with it on, and with the CPU throttled to a quarter 81 and 100 ms off, 77 and 119 ms on; placing the 104 bodies took a median of 0.1 ms and at most 0.9 ms a draw (`stats.asteroids.ms`). Nothing is drawn at rest. `stats.asteroids` says how many were placed and named. A page turns it on with the attribute:

```html
<!doctype html>
<html lang="en">
  <head>
    <meta charset="utf-8" />
    <meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1" />
    <title>The asteroids of the set, on 2 November 2026</title>
    <script type="module" src="https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/@johnmorrisdotca/wakusei-planets/space/element/+esm"></script>
  </head>
  <body>
    <wakusei-space time="2026-11-02T14:00Z" speed="0" scale="true" asteroids look="realistic" style="width: 420px"></wakusei-space>
  </body>
</html>
```

`goTo` and `ride` take an asteroid's id from `/asteroids` when the option is on: `space.goTo("itokawa")` brings the camera to a few times the asteroid's size from it (at least 200 km) looking at it, and keeps it there as the clock runs; `space.ride("apophis")` puts the camera at the asteroid, looking at the Sun with the way it goes at the top (`view: "follow"` and `distanceKm` keep a distance and look at it instead, as `goTo` does). Both are made at the true scale: the view is set to `scale="true"` if it is not already, because the squeezed scale cannot place a camera among the bodies, and going back to `compressed`, turning the option off, a `goTo` of a body or `ride(null)` ends the move and the camera goes back where it was. `space.riding.target.id` says which asteroid and `wakusei-ride` is sent as for any ride; `wakusei-hop` is not (an asteroid is no stop of the hopper). `europa` and `dione` are moons first; `asteroid:europa` and `asteroid:dione` are the forms that mean the asteroid (`asteroid:eros` works for every id). A call made before the records are in waits for them, and is dropped if another move is asked for first. With the option off, an asteroid's id throws a `RangeError` that says to turn it on, and with the option on a name that is no body of the view and no asteroid throws one that says so; with the option off a name that is neither returns `false` from `goTo`, as it always did.

```ts no-run
import type { WakuseiSpace } from "@johnmorrisdotca/wakusei-planets/space/element";

const space = document.querySelector<WakuseiSpace>("wakusei-space")!;
space.asteroids = true;
space.goTo("itokawa", { smooth: false });
console.log(space.riding?.view, space.scale);
space.ride("apophis", { smooth: false });
try {
  space.goTo("not-an-asteroid");
} catch (error) {
  console.log((error as RangeError).message);
}
```

## Every other known moon, as dots

`moons` (the attribute, `space.moons`, `space.setMoons(choice, planet)`) chooses, for each planet, between `major` (the moons the view draws in full, today's default) and `all`, which adds every other moon of [`/moon-catalogue`](moon-catalogue.md) as a small pressable dot round the planet with its orbit. `moons-of="jupiter=all saturn=major"` sets single planets, and a planet named there keeps its choice whatever `moons` says; `space.setMoons("all")` with no planet sets every planet at once and clears the choices of single planets. `moon-colors` says what the orbits' colours mean: `group` (the default: the moon's family, such as Jupiter's Ananke group or Saturn's Norse group) or `direction` (blue goes with the planet's turn, red against it); `moonOrbitKey(by)` from `/moon-catalogue` is that key as data, and `moonFamilies(planet)` lists a planet's moons by family with counts and sizes.

The dots are where the catalogue puts them, drawn nearer in the compressed scale by the same mapping as the drawn moons, so Jupiter's and Saturn's far moons make a cloud round the planet. The dots are one draw and the orbit lines another, built once and only moved with their planet unless the scale, the colouring or the month changes; nothing is drawn at rest. The catalogue arrives as its own chunk when a planet is first set to `all` or a filter is applied. `stats.allMoons` says whether any planet is set to `all`, whether the catalogue has loaded, how many dots and orbits the last draw drew and which planets show them. A page with its own subset of three.js (`setThreeLoader`) includes `Points`, `PointsMaterial`, `LineSegments` and `LineBasicMaterial`. Pressing a dot selects the moon (`wakusei-body-select`; `wakusei-body-click` and `wakusei-moon-click` carry `{ id, name, kind: "catalogue-moon", host }`) and a second press goes to its planet. The dots show each moon's orbit and roughly where on it the moon is: regular moons are within about half a degree, many small outer moons can be tens of degrees off within a year, so a dot is not an exact position. Each moon's measured error is in the catalogue (`accuracy`, `grade`). The attribute `all-moons` and `space.allMoons` still work, as `moons="all"` for every planet.

Use `space.moonFilter` or the [moon filter attributes](moon-catalogue.md#filtering-the-catalogue) to restrict both full moons and small dots. In the space demo, the All moons panel shows matching results for the planet in view. The filters apply across the space view. The numeric `moon-size` multiplier remains available.

## Going to a body, and the hopper

A first press on a body or its name selects it: `wakusei-body-select` fires with the same detail (for a page's card), `space.selected` reads it, and the view stays where it is. A second press on the selected body, or `space.goTo("saturn")`, makes it the middle of the view: the camera glides there in about a second (a jump for a reader who asked for reduced motion, or with `{ smooth: false }`), the zoom is brought to 1 so the body is whole in view (`{ zoom }` asks for another), and the view stays on it as time runs. A press never sends the view anywhere else, and a press on the body already at the middle does nothing. `goTo` returns whether the view can go there; with a `<kazoku-view-controls>` bound to the element the bar holds the zoom, so the page applies the one the event carries (`controls.controller.set({ zoom }, true, "jump")`); when `goTo` travels, `wakusei-hop` also carries `yaw` and `tilt`, the side the camera comes from (see Travelling between bodies), and the page applies them with the zoom (`controls.controller.set({ zoom, yaw, tilt }, true, "jump")`), or the way in is not straight. A zoom gesture at a stage out from the Sun is a step with the bar too: the element announces the step with `wakusei-hop` and a zoom of 1.

The hopper goes through the bodies in one order: the Sun, Mercury, Venus, the Earth, the Moon, Mars, Phobos, Deimos, Jupiter and its moons, and on outwards, each planet followed by its moons nearest first (`HOP_ORDER` from `/space` is the whole order). `space.next()` and `space.previous()` go to the next and the previous body, round from the last to the Sun, and each return the body's `BodyInfo`. Every change of the middle fires `wakusei-hop` with `{ current, previous, next }` (each a `BodyInfo`, and `zoom` when `goTo` or a hop asked for one), so two arrows can show the names of where they lead; `space.hopper` reads the same at any time. `space.bodyList()` is the jump list: every body the view can go to, in order, with its `kind` (`star`, `planet`, `dwarf-planet`, `moon`) and, for a moon, its `host`. `findBody("Saturn")` and `findBody("土星")` from `/space` read a body from what a reader types.

## Holding the camera while time runs: stars, body, pair

`frame` (an attribute, and `space.frame`) says what the camera's direction is held to while time runs. `stars`, the default, holds it fixed to the stars: the ground of the body in focus turns under the camera as it spins, and the Sun goes right round Mercury. `body` turns the camera with the body in focus, so its ground stays still under the viewer as it spins, the same idea as the Earth view's "turn with the Earth" and for any body (Ceres, Mars, a moon): `space.subCameraPoint()` stays the same point. A body with no known rotation (Halley, Nereid, Hiiaka and Namaka) is held to the stars in `body`, and `stats.frame.frame` says `stars`. `pair` turns the camera with the body in focus and a companion, so the companion stays at the same place in the picture while the two move and the camera circles the body in focus: with the Sun behind Mercury at the back left it stays there for as long as time runs. `pair-with` (an attribute, and `space.pairWith`) names the companion, a body's name; with none it is the companion the last move arrived with (`stats.companion`; a move arrives with the body it left behind the body it goes to), and with no companion a pair is held to the stars.

A drag changes the framing and the new framing is kept, in every frame: the camera turns from the direction it is looking from, the stars' direction is taken from that moment, and the frame carries the new framing on. A `goTo` or a `ride` ends a pair (the frame goes back to `stars`) unless the move arrives with a companion, which becomes the pair. In a pair the companion never passes behind the body in focus: where the camera would be straight on the far side of it the camera is moved to one side by as little as it takes, so the picture slides and never jumps. Reduced motion needs nothing of its own: a frame only changes where the camera is as the clock moves, and the clock does as the reader asked.

It costs no draw of its own: the direction is carried in the draws the running clock already makes, and at rest nothing is drawn. `stats.frame` is `{ asked, frame, pair }`: the frame asked for, the frame the last draw held, and the body a pair is held with. `wakusei-frame` is sent when the frame held changes, with `{ frame, asked, pair, previous }`. The pure parts are `FRAMES`, `readFrame`, `bodyBasis`, `hasRotation`, `pairAxis`, `pairBasis`, `carry`, `keepClear` and `directionBetween` from `/space`.

## Following the camera with a star backdrop

Listen for `wakusei-camera` on the space view to turn a page's own star backdrop with it. The first live draw sends a snapshot, and later draws send one when the camera, pan or picture size changes. The event takes no extra draw. A backdrop can also read `space.stats.camera` when it attaches after the first picture.

The snapshot's `forward` and `up` are unit vectors in equatorial J2000 scene axes. For catalogue directions, right ascension 0° is +x, right ascension 90° is −z, and the north celestial pole is +y. Use the vertical `fov` in degrees, `width` and `height` for the projection, and `panX` and `panY` to offset the picture in CSS pixels. A distant backdrop follows the orientation and projection and can leave out the camera's translation.

```ts no-run
import type { SpaceCamera, WakuseiSpace } from "@johnmorrisdotca/wakusei-planets/space/element";

function followCamera(space: WakuseiSpace, drawSky: (camera: SpaceCamera) => void): () => void {
  const update = (event: CustomEvent<SpaceCamera>) => drawSky(event.detail);
  space.addEventListener("wakusei-camera", update);
  const camera = space.stats.camera;
  if (camera.width > 0) drawSky(camera);
  return () => space.removeEventListener("wakusei-camera", update);
}
```

## Full screen

`immersive` (an attribute, and `space.immersive`) makes the element a full-screen view: it fills the box it is in, whatever the shape (it is `width: 100%; height: 100%`, and a page that gives it any width and height does the same without the attribute), and a plain wheel zooms and a drag anywhere on it turns it, a touch drag included, instead of scrolling the page. Without `immersive` kazoku-ui's rules stand: Ctrl, Command or Alt with the wheel zooms and the plain wheel scrolls the page. At zoom 1 the body looked at is framed, with a margin of about a fifth, by the shorter side of the view, in a tall window as in a wide one, so it is never cut off (Saturn is framed with its rings), and `zoom-min` takes the camera as far out as the whole solar system.

```html
<style>
  html, body { margin: 0; height: 100%; background: #04060c; }
  wakusei-space { position: fixed; inset: 0; }
</style>
<wakusei-space immersive time="now" speed="3600"></wakusei-space>
```

The page [space-full.html](https://johnmorrisdotca.github.io/wakusei-planets/space-full.html) is this, with the family's view controls over the bottom. `checkGestures` from `@johnmorrisdotca/kazoku-ui/testing` passes on it, with one named exception: the plain wheel's pan, because there the plain wheel zooms.

## Which way things go: arrows and help mode

With `orbits` on, two small arrowheads sit along each orbit line, pointing the way the body goes round (the Moon's line too): two are enough to show the way, and more is clutter. `arrows` (an attribute and a property, `space.arrows = 3`) sets how many: 2 by default, at most 4, 0 for none; anything else is made a whole number in that range (`arrowCount` reads it, `ARROWS_PER_ORBIT` and `MAX_ARROWS_PER_ORBIT` are the two numbers). The long orbits (Sedna's, Eris's, Gonggong's, Halley's) are cut at equal steps round the ellipse, not equal steps of time, so the sharp turn near the Sun is a smooth curve and not one straight stroke with corners. The arrowheads are drawn about nine CSS pixels long whatever the zoom, so they read from the whole solar system to the Moon and are never thick enough to hide a body, and they keep clear of the body itself. Close to a body (its disc a few dozen pixels across) the orbit lines are seen edge-on and would cross it as long straight scratches, so only the lines of its own system are drawn (the Moon's with the Earth's, and the Earth's with the Moon's) and, of those, the part within about twelve radii of it fades out: no line is ever drawn across the disc. `stats.orbits.hidden` lists the lines left out. The arrows show the way the body really travels, so they do not change when time runs backwards: then the body moves against its arrows. `arrowSites(line)` and `chevron(...)` from `/space` are the places and shapes, and `stats.orbits` and `stats.arrows` say where each arrow is and which way it points.

Orbit lines fade, light up and can be pressed. With `orbits` on, every line (the planets', the dwarf planets', the minor bodies', the Moon's, every catalogue moon's, the orbiters' and the Roadster's) is fainter the more lines are in the picture and the farther out the view is, and never under a sixth of its own opacity (`stats.orbits.fade` says how many lines were counted, how far out the view is, 0 to 1, and the opacity used; arrowheads fade with them). With the names on, point at a line and it is drawn over the picture at full strength with its body's name beside the pointer, and `wakusei-body-hover` fires for that body. The lit line is a layer of the view's own, so lighting it draws nothing and nothing runs at rest; the line under the pointer is worked out on a pointer move only. A press on a line, after the bodies and their names, is a press on its body: the first press selects it (`wakusei-body-select`) and the camera stays, the second goes there, and a press with Ctrl or Command goes there at once, with no glide. A catalogue moon's line is that moon's, and its second press goes to its planet. A line is reached within 6 CSS pixels for a mouse and 8 for a finger.

## Styled orbits

`setPaths(list)` (the property `paths`) draws up to 16 orbits in looks of the page's own, each `{ id, style }`. `id` is a body's name (`mars`, `io`, `土星`). A planet's, dwarf planet's or minor body's line is its trip round the Sun; a moon's is its trip round its planet. `style` is the name of a look, or part of a look `{ color, width, opacity, dash }`. A longer list keeps its last 16 (`stats.paths.dropped` counts the rest). The same body twice keeps the later look. A name that is not a body with an orbit is skipped (`stats.paths.unresolved`). `[]` takes them all away.

`setPathStyles(table)` (the property `pathStyles` is the whole table in use) changes the looks by name. The built-in ones differ by width and dash as well as colour: `selected` (white, 2 px, solid), `hovered` (white, 1.5 px, long dashes), `favourite` (amber, 1.75 px, short dashes) and `path` (white, 1.5 px, solid, the look of a path that names none). A page's entry is laid over the built-in one of its name; a new name starts from `path`; `null` takes a page's entry away. A look is `{ color, width: 0.5 to 8 CSS pixels, opacity: 0 to 1, dash: [dash, gap] in CSS pixels, or null for solid }`.

The lines are one overlay of the view, drawn when the view draws and not while it rests, and they add no graphics-card draw. They are still drawn when `orbits` is off. `path-hit` (8 px by default, 0 to 40, `off` for none; a finger has twice the reach and at least 16) makes `wakusei-path-hover` and `wakusei-path-click` say which styled path is under the pointer: `{ id, style, x, y, distancePx, pointer }`. A body under the pointer is heard first, and a styled path does not also press its body. `pathAt(x, y)` asks the same for any point. `stats.paths.drawn` is how many the last draw drew.

```ts no-run
import type { WakuseiSpace } from "@johnmorrisdotca/wakusei-planets/space/element";

const space = document.querySelector<WakuseiSpace>("wakusei-space")!;
space.setPathStyles({ favourite: { width: 3 } });
space.setPaths([{ id: "mars", style: "favourite" }, { id: "io", style: "selected" }]);
```

`help` (an attribute, and `space.help`; off by default) is the help mode: on the body looked at it draws a line along its axis and a curved arrow round it showing which way it spins. The lines have depth against their own body, so nothing is drawn through it: the axis runs into the near pole and comes out of the far pole only where that end is not behind the globe, and the part of the arrow that passes behind the globe is hidden. `help-through` (an attribute, and `space.helpThrough`; off by default) brings back the whole lines drawn over the globe. `help-place` (an attribute, and `space.helpPlace`; `pole` by default) chooses where the curved arrow sits: `pole` draws a small arrow just outside the surface, around the north-pole line, and `across` draws the wide arrow a little off the centre, across the body, which is how it was drawn before. The arrow turns by the right-hand rule about the axis the body really spins about, which `spinAxis(id, ms)` works out from how its prime meridian moves: the north pole for the Earth, Mars, Jupiter and most bodies, and the opposite way about the pole for Venus and Uranus, which turn backwards. Uranus, lying on its side, shows its axis nearly along the ecliptic. A moon that keeps one face to its planet spins once an orbit, the way it goes round. The demo ties it to the family's Help switch in its header. `stats.help` says which body, its axis and what the arrow drawn turns about.

## The belts

The view draws the asteroid belts and the Kuiper belt by default: 3,340 numbered small bodies from NASA/JPL's Small-Body Database in seven belts, each one instanced draw of its own colour. `space.belts = ["main", "trojan"]` (or the attribute `belts`) chooses them, `[]` or `belts="off"` turns them off, `space.enterBelt("main")` flies the camera into a belt and `space.leaveBelt()` comes out, with a line on the view saying how thinly the bodies really lie. [The belts](belts.md) has the table, the numbers and the server side. In the squeezed scale the view also draws the inner edge of the Oort cloud, about 2,000 au out, as a labelled shell.

## Properties, events, stats

Attributes: `center` (`"lon,lat"`), `time`, `speed`, `scale` (`true`, the default, or `compressed`), `help` (the help mode; off by default), `help-place` (`pole` or `across`; `pole` by default), `frame` (`stars`, `body` or `pair`), `pair-with`, `look` (`realistic` or `painted`), `light`, `labels` and `orbits` (`off` hides them), `path-hit` (how close a pointer must be to a styled orbit, 8 px by default, `off` for none), `moons` (`major` or `all`: every known moon of a planet as a pressable dot with its orbit), `moons-of` (single planets: `jupiter=all`), `moon-colors` (`group` or `direction`), `belts` (which belts are drawn; `off` for none), `lang`, `texture-base`, `texture-far-base`, `max-texture`, `tiles` (where the finer map tiles are; `off` for none), `far-opacity` (the strength of the far names, 0.5 by default). Events: `wakusei-view` (the view changed: `yaw`, `tilt`, `zoom`), `wakusei-ready`, `wakusei-unavailable`, `wakusei-state`, `wakusei-timechange` (the date, four times a second at most while time runs, and once when it holds: `{ time, running }`), `wakusei-time` (every step the clock takes), `wakusei-body-click`, `wakusei-body-focus`, `wakusei-body-hover` and `wakusei-body-hover-end` (a body under the pointer, a finger or the focus, and none), `wakusei-path-hover` and `wakusei-path-click` (a styled orbit under the pointer, and a press on one), `wakusei-sight` (bodies in view and out of it), `wakusei-frame` (the frame the camera is held in changed). `belts`, `enterBelt`, `leaveBelt` and `flyingThrough` are described in the section above. `stats` has `state`, `draws`, `frame`, `contexts`, `canvas`, `distance`, `time`, `lookups`, `camera`, `bodies` and `tiles`.

## Travelling between bodies

`goTo` and `ride` move the camera from the view it has to the view at the new body along a path worked out once at the start (`travelPose` from `/space`, a pure function of time): `transition` (an attribute, `space.transition`, and an option of each call) names it. `turn-travel`, the default, is one continuous curve and never an L: the camera turns beside the body being left until the next body is in the picture (a point or a marked dot when it is too small to see), and goes on to it, with it in the picture the whole way, growing, without stopping or turning a corner where the one gives way to the other (the way on starts while the turn is still going, as far as that costs the picture nothing; `plan.blend` says how much of the turn it overlaps, and the move is shorter by the time they share). Where the body left and the destination can share the picture the camera backs up a little, until the body left keeps about half of the picture's height, and stands so that the body left is large on one side and the destination small on the other, on whichever side needs less movement; where the destination is not in the picture it is the least shift that brings it in. The look direction turns one way only, the picture does not show an empty frame (see below for the one place it still can), and the camera never flies through a planet, dwarf planet, small body, the Sun or the Moon: it keeps `TRAVEL_CLEARANCE` (0.35) radii above the surface, and a body near the way is passed as close as that, not routed round (the moons of the other planets are not looked for). Every body is taken where the clock puts it as the move starts and where it puts it as the move ends, so a clock that runs a game hour a second does not move the Moon out from under the camera. Two moons of one planet, or a planet and its moon, keep the planet in the picture and go round it. Where the views of two bodies of one planet's family leave no way round the planet that keeps either of them in the picture, the camera backs off from the body left until both are in the picture, turns, and comes in, when that can be done within the pace limits of the turn. A move from far out (the Earth seen from the distance the Earth view hands over at, a few pixels across) to a planet, the Sun or a dwarf planet does not back off to a view of the whole system and in again: where the stand that keeps the last view's own look direction would be more than 1.2 times as far from the body left as the camera began (`BACK_UP`), the camera goes straight at the destination with it in the picture and the body left shrinking behind, and the look direction is turned round the destination only over the last stretch, as long as that leaves no fault in the walk that the other way did not have (otherwise the other way stands). Where the straight way in would pass close to the planet, the camera stands beyond the destination, on the side away from the planet, and comes in from there, so the destination does not swing across the picture. The search for such a way is limited to about 60 ms (`TRAVEL_BUDGET_MS`), so on a slow device a move may be planned with fewer of the other ways tried; a page that calls `travelPlan` itself gets no limit unless it passes `budget` (a clock and a number of milliseconds), and must make a new input for each move: a move is planned once for the input it is given, and the input is frozen when it is planned. A few such moves, between moons seen whole from the side the camera comes from, still pass through a stretch of empty picture (1 of 360 walks of six pairs of moons from ten sides, 15 of 86,760 samples). `direct` goes straight at the destination, eased; `look-back` goes straight while watching the body left; `auto` is `direct` when the destination is on the screen as the move starts, `turn-travel` when it is not; `cut` jumps, which is what a reader who asked for reduced motion always gets. The lit side wins, and the body being left stays in the picture for as long as it can: the move holds it in the picture through the turn and on the way as long as the way allows (a planet and its moon stay together all through), and where the body gone to would still be at least half lit (`BEHIND_LIT`, one half, the same as the rule for every other arrival) the camera slows near the destination and comes round it, so that the body left is in the last view behind the destination at its back left or right (above or below it in a tall picture), clear of the disc, and, where a side does both, with one of the body's moons in the picture too and named as the companion (`behind` in `travelArrival` and `arrivalSide`; a near-square picture has no room beside the disc). Where keeping it behind would show the night side, which is every move outward to a planet beyond the body left (the Earth to Jupiter or Saturn, the Sun to a planet), the camera arrives on the sunlit side with one of the body's moons in the picture, and the body left is not in the last view. A move arrives from the side it comes from, moved so a companion stays in the picture: its own moons, or its planet when it is a moon; otherwise the body it came from, or the nearest planet in that direction; otherwise the Sun, and only off to one side so at least half the body stays lit. A lit globe is never given up for a black one. The companion keeps its name. The controls' yaw and tilt are set to that side, and a turn the reader makes afterwards is left alone. A double click, which jumps the zoom to Fit or to twice as close, chooses the side again. A straight move (`direct`, `look-back`) keeps its destination in the picture the whole way, so it is not swung to a companion's side. A move asked for while another is going starts from the camera's place at that moment, with no jump. A move asked for in the same breath as another, before the first has drawn (a page that calls `ride(null)` and then `ride(id)`), leaves the body the camera is at, not the one the first was going to. How long a move takes comes from how far the picture changes, between 1.2 and 9 seconds (a move whose way on starts during its turn is shorter by the time they share), times `pace` (an attribute and `space.pace`, 1 by default; 2 is twice as slow); `duration` in milliseconds on a call sets one move's length instead. `stats.travelling` reports the move at the last draw (its kind and path, its stage, how far along it is, and whether the body left and the one gone to are in the picture), or null when the camera is still.

When the ordinary horizon would turn over near a pole, the travel camera tips and pans in turn, giving the pole at least ten degrees of room. The roll the reader sets carries through the move.

## Stepping out from the Sun

Backing out from the Sun goes by stages a reader knows, each framed so that every orbit of it is in the picture: `inner-planets` (Mercury to Mars), `asteroid-belt` (Vesta, Ceres and Pallas), `outer-planets` (Jupiter to Neptune), `kuiper-belt` (Pluto and the dwarf planets out to Eris) and `whole-system` (every orbit the view draws). `space.stepOut()` goes one stage further out (the first stage from any body) and `space.stepIn()` one stage back in, to the Sun itself from the first; each is the same travel move as `goTo`, a straight pull back or push in, and returns the stage reached (or null when there is none). `goTo` takes a stage's id too. At a stage the controls hold zoom 1 and a zoom gesture is taken as a step, out or in, one step a gesture (zoom events less than 300 milliseconds apart are one gesture, so a pinch that goes on is one step, and the next gesture after a pause is another); a drag still turns the view. The stage's name is shown in the corner of the view in the element's language (`part="region"` styles it); `space.region` is the stage the view is at or null, `stats.region` adds its name and how far it reaches from the Sun and how far back the camera stands, and `wakusei-region` is sent when a stage is reached. The stages and their orbit lines are `SUN_REGIONS`, `regionOrbits` and `regionView` from `/space`.

## Riding along

`space.ride("moon")`, `space.ride("io")` or `space.ride({ at })` puts the camera on a moving point and looks at the body it goes round: see [Riding along](ride-along.md).

Beside a moon, north is up, always: the picture's up is the planet's own north pole laid across the line of sight (`stats.camera.up` reports it, and `stats.camera.forward` where the camera looks), whatever the date and wherever the Sun is, so the planet's equator and rings stay level, and both discs are framed above or beside each other as that leaves them. Where the Sun lies near the line from the planet through the moon (within about 20 degrees), the camera goes round over the planet's north side a little at a time rather than hopping to the Sun's other side. One moment stays sharp: with the Sun about 11 degrees south of the moon's orbit, the camera's side swings round within a few frames as the moon crosses the line from the Sun through its planet (Saturn's and Neptune's moons meet it for a season every few years; Jupiter's never do).

## The budget

The view keeps the same rules as every live 3D view in the package, and `pnpm test:freeze` proves them: one WebGL context through the shared renderer, a canvas inside 4096 pixels a side and 6 million pixels, nothing drawn at rest, at most 30 frames a second, real time redrawn once a minute, nothing drawn out of sight, everything freed when the element goes, and a lost context recovered.
