## The numbers

No picture is used by the line and shaded looks: they are drawn from numbers.

- **Sizes, flattening, axial tilt, poles and rotation**: the IAU Working Group on Cartographic Coordinates and Rotational Elements, B. A. Archinal et al. (2018), "Report of the IAU Working Group on Cartographic Coordinates and Rotational Elements: 2015", Celestial Mechanics and Dynamical Astronomy 130:22, and NASA's planetary and satellite fact sheets (https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/, read 2026-11-02). Facts are not copyrightable; the values were typed from these sources.
- **Mercury to Neptune**: the VSOP87B series, P. Bretagnon and G. Francou (1988), "Planetary theories in rectangular and spherical variables: VSOP87 solutions", Astronomy and Astrophysics 202, 309, from the files of IMCCE, Observatoire de Paris (https://ftp.imcce.fr/pub/ephem/planets/vsop87/, read 2026-11-07), cut to 991 of 35,080 terms into `src/data/vsop.ts` by `scripts/make-vsop-data.py`. The files state their precision and ask to be cited; they carry no licence text, and the numbers are a published scientific table.
- **Time scales**: the leap seconds are IERS Bulletin C; delta T before 1972 is the polynomials of F. Espenak and J. Meeus (NASA, https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/SEhelp/deltatpoly2004.html), in `src/time.ts`.
- **Calendars, sidereal time and Mars time (`/time`)**: written here from J. Meeus, "Astronomical Algorithms" (2nd edition, chapters 7 and 12: the Julian day and the sidereal time), the Richards day-number algorithm for the calendars, M. Allison and M. McEwen, "A post-Pathfinder evaluation of aerocentric solar coordinates with improved timing recipes for Mars seasonal/diurnal climate studies", Planetary and Space Science 48, 215 (2000), and Meeus's two-term formula for TDB minus TT. The names of the Julian-date functions follow the `julian` package (MIT, Stepan Stolyarov), whose code is not used; `julian` 0.2.0 and astronomy-engine are development-time comparisons in `test/calendar.test.ts` only and are not shipped.
- **Pluto's position**: a Chebyshev table (`src/data/pluto.ts`, made by `scripts/make-pluto-data.py`) fitted to JPL Horizons (https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/api/horizons.api, read 2026-11-07), 1800 to 2200; outside those years, and for its earlier edition, JPL Solar System Dynamics, "Keplerian Elements for Approximate Positions of the Major Planets", Table 1, 1800 AD to 2050 AD (https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/planets/approx_pos.html, read 2026-11-02; Pluto from the same page's earlier edition). NASA/JPL content is not subject to copyright in the United States; credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech.
- **Moons' orbits**: fitted to the positions and velocities of JPL Horizons (https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/api/horizons.api, read 2026-11-07) for 1950 to 2050, by `scripts/make-moon-data.py`, which writes `src/data/orbits.ts`; the Moon's row, and the pole of each moon's reference plane, are from JPL Solar System Dynamics, "Planetary Satellite Mean Elements" (https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/sats/elem/, read 2026-11-02). The positions the accuracy test compares with are in `test/data/horizons.json`, fetched by `scripts/fetch-horizons-reference.py`. Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech; the satellite ephemerides behind Horizons are R. A. Jacobson and colleagues' (the page names each).
- **The Moon's position**: the lunar solution ELP2000-82B, M. Chapront-Touzé and J. Chapront, "The lunar ephemeris ELP 2000", Astron. Astrophys. 124, 50 (1983) and "ELP 2000-85", Astron. Astrophys. 190, 342 (1988), from IMCCE, Observatoire de Paris (https://ftp.imcce.fr/pub/ephem/moon/elp82b/). Its 37,872 terms are cut to the 540 above 0.05 arc-seconds (`scripts/make-lunar-data.py`, `src/data/lunar.ts`) and written out as numbers, with the constants fitted to JPL's DE200 that the notice gives. IMCCE's files carry no licence text; they are published for use with the credit above (the same footing as VSOP87).
- **Sun and Moon times (`/suncalc`)**: written here from the standard methods: J. Meeus, "Astronomical Algorithms" (2nd edition, chapters 11, 12, 21, 22, 48), the IAU 1976 precession, the IAU 1980 nutation (its largest terms), Saemundsson's refraction formula, and the Naval Observatory's convention for the rise and set of the Moon and Sun. The names, arguments and results follow the public interface of suncalc (BSD-2-Clause, Copyright (c) Volodymyr Agafonkin; https://github.com/mourner/suncalc), whose code is not used. suncalc 1.9.0 and 2.1.2 and astronomy-engine (MIT) are development-time comparisons in `test/suncalc.test.ts` only and are not shipped.
- **Stars (`/stars`)**: the Yale Bright Star Catalogue, D. Hoffleit and W. H. Warren Jr. (1991), "The Bright Star Catalogue, 5th Revised Edition (Preliminary Version)", Astronomical Data Center (NASA Goddard Space Flight Center) and the Centre de Données astronomiques de Strasbourg, catalogue V/50 (https://cdsarc.cds.unistra.fr/viz-bin/cat/V/50, read through the VizieR mirror at Harvard 2026-11-07), the 5,080 stars to visual magnitude 6.0, written out as numbers by `scripts/make-star-data.py` into `src/data/stars.ts`. The catalogue's own documentation calls it public domain and the Astronomical Data Center asks that it and the authors be acknowledged, which this line does; there is no formal licence text, so this footing is informal. The common names are our own list after the IAU Working Group on Star Names (https://www.iau.org/science/scientific_bodies/working_groups/280/); the star colour is Ballesteros's B-V to temperature relation (F. J. Ballesteros 2012, EPL 97, 34008) and a piecewise black-body colour fit. Deliberately NOT used: the Hipparcos, Tycho and Gaia catalogues (ESA, CC BY-NC 3.0 IGO, non-commercial), and HYG, AT-HYG and OpenNGC (CC BY-SA, which would put share-alike terms on this package's data).
- **Mass and escape speed** (`JOURNEY_STOPS`): JPL "Planetary Satellite Physical Parameters" (https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/sats/phys_par/, read 2026-11-04) for the GM of the Earth, Mars through Pluto and the moons on that page; NASA's Sun, Mercury and Venus fact sheets (https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/, read 2026-11-04) for those three GM values and the Sun's escape speed; JPL "Planetary Physical Parameters" (https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/planets/phys_par.html, read 2026-11-04) for the planets' escape speeds and for the masses of Ceres, Eris, Makemake and Haumea, turned into GM with the CODATA 2018 G that page names. A moon's escape speed is computed from its GM and the radius already in this package. Nereid, Orcus, Quaoar, Gonggong, Sedna, Vanth, Hiʻiaka, Namaka and Dysnomia are left blank: those pages do not print a mass.

## The dwarf planets

Ceres, Orcus, Haumea, Quaoar, Makemake, Gonggong, Eris and Sedna (Pluto is with the planets) and the moons Vanth, Hiʻiaka, Namaka and Dysnomia. Values read on 2026-11-03.

- **Where they are**: JPL Horizons (https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/api/horizons.api), fitted as Chebyshev tables from the solar system's barycentre (`scripts/make-dwarf-data.py`, `src/data/dwarfs.ts`), with the Sun's swing about the barycentre worked out from the planets' masses (IAU 2012 and JPL DE440 mass ratios): Ceres 1900 to 2100, the others 1800 to 2200, each within 0.2 arc-seconds of Horizons. Outside those years, NASA/JPL Small-Body Database osculating elements at JD 2461200.5 (https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/api/sbdb.api), run as a plain ellipse (`src/data/dwarf.ts`): good for a few years either side of June 2026.
- **Sizes and spin**: Ceres (966.2 x 962.0 x 891.8 km) and its pole: Ermakov et al. 2017 (as listed in Wikipedia's Ceres article); its rotation: JPL SBDB (9.074170 h). Haumea: Ortiz et al. 2017, Nature 550:219 (semi-axes 1161 x 852 x 513 km), rotation 3.915341 h from Santos-Sanz et al. 2017, A&A 604:A95, pole from Kondratyev & Kornoukhov 2018, MNRAS 478:3159 (RA 282.6, Dec -13.0). Eris: radius 1163 km, Sicardy et al. 2011; it keeps one face to Dysnomia, so its rotation is Dysnomia's orbital period, Szakats et al. 2023, A&A 669:L3. Makemake: radius 715 km, Brown 2013; rotation 22.8266 h, Hromakina et al. 2019. Gonggong: radius 615 km and rotation 22.4 h, Kiss et al. 2019, Icarus. Quaoar: 1241.4 x 1093.0 x 1023.0 km and 17.6788 h, Margoti et al. 2026 and Pereira et al. 2023. Sedna: radius 453 km (Lellouch et al. 2013, TNOs are Cool IX, which allows far more) and 10.273 h, Gaudi et al. 2005. Orcus: radius 455 km, Brown & Butler 2018, rotation 13.188 h (JPL SBDB; 7 to 21 h in the literature). Wikipedia's articles on each body were the finding aid; the papers are the sources.
- **Moons' orbits**: Dysnomia, Holler et al. 2021, Icarus 355:114130, Table 2 (arXiv 2009.13733). Hiʻiaka and Namaka, Ragozzine & Brown 2009, AJ 137:4766, Table 2 (osculating elements of 2008; the two moons pull on each other, so the position years later is rough). Vanth, Brown et al. 2010, AJ 139:2700, Table 2, solution 1 of two mirror solutions. Radii: Dysnomia 308 km (Brown & Butler 2023), Vanth 221 km (Sickafoose et al. 2018, occultation), Hiʻiaka and Namaka from the Haumea article's listed diameters (370 and 150 km). Weywot, Xiangliu and Makemake's moon S/2015 (136472) 1 are left out: no orbit with a published phase.
- **Estimated, not measured** (the pictures say so only here): these bodies' poles are not measured, so each is drawn with its spin axis along its orbit's pole (Quaoar, Makemake, Gonggong, Sedna), along Dysnomia's orbit pole (Eris) or along the far end of Vanth's orbit pole, so that it spins forwards (Orcus). Hiʻiaka's and Namaka's spin is unknown and they are drawn still. Dysnomia and Vanth are assumed to keep one face to their planet. Every drawn feature on the line look of a dwarf planet except Ceres's Occator crater and Haumea's long axis is a generic crater, and so is each shaded (realistic) look: only the colour (from the published colour indices and albedos) and the sizes are from numbers. Ceres, Haumea and Dysnomia's shapes are from the sources above. Only Ceres has a painted look.

The tests compare the Galilean moons and four planets with astronomy-engine (MIT, https://github.com/cosinekitty/astronomy), a development dependency that is never shipped.

## The Earth

The Earth's line drawing is chikyu-earth's own globe (MIT, by John Morris; https://github.com/johnmorrisdotca/chikyu-earth), an optional peer dependency: its own NOTICE.md has the terms of its outlines. The Earth's realistic and painted looks are drawn here, like every other body, from NASA's Blue Marble picture (below).
