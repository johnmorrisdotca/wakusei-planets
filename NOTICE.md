## The numbers

No picture is used by the line and shaded looks: they are drawn from numbers.

- **Sizes, flattening, axial tilt, poles and rotation**: the IAU Working Group on Cartographic Coordinates and Rotational Elements, B. A. Archinal et al. (2018), "Report of the IAU Working Group on Cartographic Coordinates and Rotational Elements: 2015", Celestial Mechanics and Dynamical Astronomy 130:22, and NASA's planetary and satellite fact sheets (https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/, read 2026-11-02). Facts are not copyrightable; the values were typed from these sources.
- **Mercury to Neptune**: the VSOP87B series, P. Bretagnon and G. Francou (1988), "Planetary theories in rectangular and spherical variables: VSOP87 solutions", Astronomy and Astrophysics 202, 309, from the files of IMCCE, Observatoire de Paris (https://ftp.imcce.fr/pub/ephem/planets/vsop87/, read 2026-11-07), cut to 991 of 35,080 terms into `src/data/vsop.ts` by `scripts/make-vsop-data.py`. The files state their precision and ask to be cited; they carry no licence text, and the numbers are a published scientific table.
- **Time scales**: the leap seconds are IERS Bulletin C; delta T before 1972 is the polynomials of F. Espenak and J. Meeus (NASA, https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/SEhelp/deltatpoly2004.html), in `src/time.ts`.
- **Calendars, sidereal time and Mars time (`/time`)**: written here from J. Meeus, "Astronomical Algorithms" (2nd edition, chapters 7 and 12: the Julian day and the sidereal time), the Richards day-number algorithm for the calendars, M. Allison and M. McEwen, "A post-Pathfinder evaluation of aerocentric solar coordinates with improved timing recipes for Mars seasonal/diurnal climate studies", Planetary and Space Science 48, 215 (2000), and Meeus's two-term formula for TDB minus TT. The names of the Julian-date functions follow the `julian` package (MIT, Stepan Stolyarov), whose code is not used; `julian` 0.2.0 and astronomy-engine are development-time comparisons in `test/calendar.test.ts` only and are not shipped.
- **Pluto's position**: a Chebyshev table (`src/data/pluto.ts`, made by `scripts/make-pluto-data.py`) fitted to JPL Horizons (https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/api/horizons.api, read 2026-11-07), 1800 to 2200; outside those years, and for its earlier edition, JPL Solar System Dynamics, "Keplerian Elements for Approximate Positions of the Major Planets", Table 1, 1800 AD to 2050 AD (https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/planets/approx_pos.html, read 2026-11-02; Pluto from the same page's earlier edition). NASA/JPL content is not subject to copyright in the United States; credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech.
- **Moons' orbits**: fitted to the positions and velocities of JPL Horizons (https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/api/horizons.api, read 2026-11-07) for 1800 to 2199, by `scripts/make-moon-data.py`, which writes `src/data/orbits.ts`; the Moon's row, and the pole of each moon's reference plane, are from JPL Solar System Dynamics, "Planetary Satellite Mean Elements" (https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/sats/elem/, read 2026-11-02). The positions the accuracy test compares with are in `test/data/horizons.json`, fetched by `scripts/fetch-horizons-reference.py`. Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech; the satellite ephemerides behind Horizons are R. A. Jacobson and colleagues' (the page names each).
- **The Moon's position**: the lunar solution ELP2000-82B, M. Chapront-Touzé and J. Chapront, "The lunar ephemeris ELP 2000", Astron. Astrophys. 124, 50 (1983) and "ELP 2000-85", Astron. Astrophys. 190, 342 (1988), from IMCCE, Observatoire de Paris (https://ftp.imcce.fr/pub/ephem/moon/elp82b/). Its 37,872 terms are cut to the 540 above 0.05 arc-seconds (`scripts/make-lunar-data.py`, `src/data/lunar.ts`) and written out as numbers, with the constants fitted to JPL's DE200 that the notice gives. IMCCE's files carry no licence text; they are published for use with the credit above (the same footing as VSOP87).
- **Jupiter's four big moons (`tables/galilean`)**: the theory L1.2, V. Lainey, L. Duriez and A. Vienne (2006), "Synthetic representation of the Galilean satellites' orbital motions from L1 ephemerides", Astron. Astrophys. 456, 783, from the series `BisL1.2.dat` that IMCCE, Observatoire de Paris publishes (https://ftp.imcce.fr/pub/ephem/satel/galilean/L1/L1.2/, read 2026-11-08), cut to 140 terms (`scripts/make-galilean-data.py`, `src/data/galilean.ts`; the arrangement of `src/theories/galilean.ts` follows IMCCE's `L1.2.f` and is checked against its `TestL1.2.res`). The README of that directory states no licence; the numbers are a published scientific table, credited here.
- **Saturn's seven inner moons (`tables/saturn-moons`)**: the theory TASS1.7, A. Vienne and L. Duriez (1995), "TASS1.6: Ephemerides of the major Saturnian satellites", Astron. Astrophys. 297, 588, from IMCCE (https://ftp.imcce.fr/pub/ephem/satel/tass17/). The terms are read from `Src/Celbody/Satsat/tass17.dat` in Orbiter (https://github.com/orbitersim/orbiter, read 2026-11-08), the same table in one plain file, MIT licence: Copyright (c) 2000-2026 Martin Schweiger. The MIT notice of Orbiter follows. Cut to 316 terms (`scripts/make-tass17-data.py`, `src/data/tass17.ts`); `src/theories/tass17.ts` follows Orbiter's `Tass17.cpp` (a conversion of IMCCE's FORTRAN) and is checked against the values in IMCCE's `tass17.f`.
- **The Uranus, Mars and Neptune systems' moons** are still fitted to Horizons. IMCCE's GUST86 (Laskar and Jacobson 1987) and ESAPHO/ESADEI (Chapront-Touzé 1990) come with a notice that forbids redistributing changed files, so they are not used; nothing of them is in this package.
- **Sun and Moon times (`/suncalc`)**: written here from the standard methods: J. Meeus, "Astronomical Algorithms" (2nd edition, chapters 11, 12, 21, 22, 48), the IAU 1976 precession, the IAU 1980 nutation (its largest terms), Saemundsson's refraction formula, and the Naval Observatory's convention for the rise and set of the Moon and Sun. The names, arguments and results follow the public interface of suncalc (BSD-2-Clause, Copyright (c) Volodymyr Agafonkin; https://github.com/mourner/suncalc), whose code is not used. suncalc 1.9.0 and 2.1.2 and astronomy-engine (MIT) are development-time comparisons in `test/suncalc.test.ts` only and are not shipped.
- **Stars (`/stars`)**: the Yale Bright Star Catalogue, D. Hoffleit and W. H. Warren Jr. (1991), "The Bright Star Catalogue, 5th Revised Edition (Preliminary Version)", Astronomical Data Center (NASA Goddard Space Flight Center) and the Centre de Données astronomiques de Strasbourg, catalogue V/50, as NASA's HEASARC serves it (table bsc5p, https://heasarc.gsfc.nasa.gov/W3Browse/star-catalog/bsc5p.html, file heasarc_bsc5p.tdat.gz, read 2026-11-08; a US government source), the 5,080 stars to visual magnitude 6.0, written out as numbers by `scripts/make-star-data.py` into `src/data/stars.ts`. The catalogue's own documentation calls it public domain and the Astronomical Data Center asks that it and the authors be acknowledged, which this line does; there is no formal licence text, so this footing is informal. The common names are our own list after the IAU Working Group on Star Names (https://www.iau.org/science/scientific_bodies/working_groups/280/); the star colour is Ballesteros's B-V to temperature relation (F. J. Ballesteros 2012, EPL 97, 34008) and a piecewise black-body colour fit. Deliberately NOT used: the Hipparcos, Tycho and Gaia catalogues (ESA, CC BY-NC 3.0 IGO, non-commercial), and HYG, AT-HYG and OpenNGC (CC BY-SA, which would put share-alike terms on this package's data).
- **Mass and escape speed** (`JOURNEY_STOPS`): JPL "Planetary Satellite Physical Parameters" (https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/sats/phys_par/, read 2026-11-04) for the GM of the Earth, Mars through Pluto and the moons on that page; NASA's Sun, Mercury and Venus fact sheets (https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/, read 2026-11-04) for those three GM values and the Sun's escape speed; JPL "Planetary Physical Parameters" (https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/planets/phys_par.html, read 2026-11-04) for the planets' escape speeds and for the masses of Ceres, Eris, Makemake and Haumea, turned into GM with the CODATA 2018 G that page names. A moon's escape speed is computed from its GM and the radius already in this package. Nereid, Orcus, Quaoar, Gonggong, Sedna, Vanth, Hiʻiaka, Namaka and Dysnomia are left blank: those pages do not print a mass.

## The dwarf planets

Ceres, Orcus, Haumea, Quaoar, Makemake, Gonggong, Eris and Sedna (Pluto is with the planets) and the moons Vanth, Hiʻiaka, Namaka and Dysnomia. Values read on 2026-11-03.

- **Where they are**: JPL Horizons (https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/api/horizons.api), fitted as Chebyshev tables from the solar system's barycentre (`scripts/make-dwarf-data.py`, `src/data/dwarfs.ts`), with the Sun's swing about the barycentre worked out from the planets' masses (IAU 2012 and JPL DE440 mass ratios): Ceres 1900 to 2100, the others 1800 to 2200, each within 0.2 arc-seconds of Horizons. Outside those years, NASA/JPL Small-Body Database osculating elements at JD 2461200.5 (https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/api/sbdb.api), run as a plain ellipse (`src/data/dwarf.ts`): good for a few years either side of June 2026.
- **Sizes and spin**: Ceres (966.2 x 962.0 x 891.8 km) and its pole: Ermakov et al. 2017 (as listed in Wikipedia's Ceres article); its rotation: JPL SBDB (9.074170 h). Haumea: Ortiz et al. 2017, Nature 550:219 (semi-axes 1161 x 852 x 513 km), rotation 3.915341 h from Santos-Sanz et al. 2017, A&A 604:A95, pole from Kondratyev & Kornoukhov 2018, MNRAS 478:3159 (RA 282.6, Dec -13.0). Eris: radius 1163 km, Sicardy et al. 2011; it keeps one face to Dysnomia, so its rotation is Dysnomia's orbital period, Szakats et al. 2023, A&A 669:L3. Makemake: radius 715 km, Brown 2013; rotation 22.8266 h, Hromakina et al. 2019. Gonggong: radius 615 km and rotation 22.4 h, Kiss et al. 2019, Icarus. Quaoar: 1241.4 x 1093.0 x 1023.0 km and 17.6788 h, Margoti et al. 2026 and Pereira et al. 2023. Sedna: radius 453 km (Lellouch et al. 2013, TNOs are Cool IX, which allows far more) and 10.273 h, Gaudi et al. 2005. Orcus: radius 455 km, Brown & Butler 2018, rotation 13.188 h (JPL SBDB; 7 to 21 h in the literature). Wikipedia's articles on each body were the finding aid; the papers are the sources.
- **Moons' orbits**: Dysnomia, Holler et al. 2021, Icarus 355:114130, Table 2 (arXiv 2009.13733). Hiʻiaka and Namaka, Ragozzine & Brown 2009, AJ 137:4766, Table 2 (osculating elements of 2008; the two moons pull on each other, so the position years later is rough). Vanth, Brown et al. 2010, AJ 139:2700, Table 2, solution 1 of two mirror solutions. Radii: Dysnomia 308 km (Brown & Butler 2023), Vanth 221 km (Sickafoose et al. 2018, occultation), Hiʻiaka and Namaka from the Haumea article's listed diameters (370 and 150 km). Weywot, Xiangliu and Makemake's moon S/2015 (136472) 1 are left out: no orbit with a published phase.
- **Estimated, not measured** (the pictures say so only here): these bodies' poles are not measured, so each is drawn with its spin axis along its orbit's pole (Quaoar, Makemake, Gonggong, Sedna), along Dysnomia's orbit pole (Eris) or along the far end of Vanth's orbit pole, so that it spins forwards (Orcus). Hiʻiaka's and Namaka's spin is unknown and they are drawn still. Dysnomia and Vanth are assumed to keep one face to their planet. Every drawn feature on the line look of a dwarf planet except Ceres's Occator crater and Haumea's long axis is a generic crater, and so is each shaded (realistic) look: only the colour (from the published colour indices and albedos) and the sizes are from numbers. Ceres, Haumea and Dysnomia's shapes are from the sources above. Only Ceres has a painted look.

The tests compare the Galilean moons and four planets with astronomy-engine (MIT, https://github.com/cosinekitty/astronomy), a development dependency that is never shipped.

## The minor bodies

Vesta, Pallas, Chiron, Pholus and Halley's comet (`MINOR_IDS`). Values read on 2026-11-10 from JPL's Small-Body Database (https://ssd-api.jpl.nasa.gov/sbdb.api) and Horizons (https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/api/horizons.api); both are NASA/JPL, not subject to copyright in the United States (credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech).

- **Where they are**: JPL Horizons, fitted as Chebyshev tables from the solar system's barycentre (`scripts/make-minor-data.py`, `src/tables/minor.ts`) like the dwarf planets, with the Sun's swing about the barycentre worked out from the planets' masses: Vesta and Pallas 1900 to 2100, Chiron, Pholus and Halley 1800 to 2200, each within 1 arc-second of Horizons on the samples left out of each fit (0.6 at the worst on the 12 dates of `test/data/horizons.json`, one request a date). Halley's segments are of unequal length (43 days near the Sun, 16 years far from it). Halley is Horizons' record 90000030, the solution of the 1986 apparition (observations 1835 to 1994): a comet's orbit past that arc is a prediction, and its non-gravitational forces are in the solution. Outside the tables' years each runs on Horizons' osculating elements (Vesta 2020-01-01, Pallas 2017-04-27, Chiron 2017-06-12, Pholus 2010-06-22, Halley 1968-01-20; `src/data/minor.ts`) as a plain ellipse: a few degrees off a century from the epoch, and Pallas, a planet's pull on an orbit tipped 35 degrees, most.
- **Sizes and spin**, as the Small-Body Database gives them: Vesta, 569.24 x 554.48 x 452.66 km, pole RA 309.0611 and Dec 42.2324, rotation 5.3421276 h, GM 17.2882844 km^3/s^2, Park et al. 2025, Nature Astronomy (DOI 10.1038/s41550-025-02533-7); its prime meridian at J2000 (285.39 degrees, the Claudia system of Dawn's maps) is the IAU's, Archinal et al. 2018, Celestial Mechanics and Dynamical Astronomy 130:22. Pallas, 568 x 532 x 448 km, Marsset et al. 2020, Nature Astronomy 4:569; pole from Carry et al. 2010, Icarus 205:460 (ecliptic longitude 30, latitude -16, which is RA 33, Dec -3), rotation 7.8132214 h; GM 13.63 km^3/s^2, Vernazza et al. 2021, A&A 654:A56. Chiron, a diameter of 166 km (an occultation, listed by the Small-Body Database as "Dunham occultation list: Duham 1998"), rotation 5.918 h, Warner et al. 2009 (the Lightcurve Database). Pholus, a diameter of 190 km, Earth, Moon, and Planets 89:117 (2002), rotation 9.98 h, Lightcurve Database. Halley's nucleus, 14.9 x 8.2 km, Keller et al. 1987, A&A 187:807 (the Giotto flyby), radius 5.5 km (the Small-Body Database). Colours from the published albedos and spectral types (Vesta, V-type, albedo 0.42; Pallas, B-type, 0.155; Chiron, Cb, 0.15; Pholus, very red, 0.044; Halley, 0.04).
- **Vesta's picture** is Dawn's own: the global photomap on Wikimedia Commons (public domain; table above). The other four have never been imaged as more than a few pixels, except Halley, whose nucleus Giotto photographed in 1986 (ESA; those pictures' terms keep them out of this package): their shaded looks and line drawings are generic craters on the numbers' shape, which this paragraph says.
- **Estimated, not measured** (the pictures say so only here): Chiron's and Pholus's poles are not measured, so each is drawn with its spin axis along its orbit's pole (RA 258.793, Dec 72.317 for Chiron; RA 334.495, Dec 66.246 for Pholus). Halley's nucleus tumbles (about 2.2 days, a period this package does not model): it is drawn still, its axis along its orbit's pole. Vesta's and Pallas's features on the line look (Vesta's Rheasilvia basin at the south pole, and two bands for its equatorial troughs) are Dawn's; the craters are generic.

## The size line-up's radii

The twelve stars of `<wakusei-lineup>` (`src/lineup/data.ts`, `LINEUP_STARS`). Every radius and temperature was typed from a refereed paper (the abstract and the tables, on arXiv and in the journal's own record, read on 2026-11-09); the papers are credited and none of their text, figures or tables is copied. Facts are not copyrightable. A radius is an angular diameter that was measured (interferometry where it exists) times a distance, or, where the row says so, a radius worked out from brightness. Nothing is taken from an infographic, from Wikipedia's text, or from the catalogues of Hipparcos, Gaia or Tycho (ESA, CC BY-NC 3.0 IGO): where a paper's distance is a catalogue parallax (Vega, Arcturus, Aldebaran, Rigel, Antares), only the paper's published radius and angle are typed here, never the parallax. Each ADS bibcode below opens the paper at https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/<bibcode>/abstract.

- **The Sun**: the IAU's nominal radius, 695,700 km, and effective temperature, 5,772 K: I. Prša et al. (2016), "Nominal values for selected solar and planetary quantities: IAU 2015 Resolution B3", AJ 152, 41 (2016AJ....152...41P).
- **Sirius A**: radius 1.7144 ± 0.0090 Suns and 9,845 K, H. E. Bond et al. (2017), "The Sirius System and its Astrophysical Puzzles", ApJ 840, 70 (2017ApJ...840...70B), from the angular diameter 6.048 ± 0.040 mas of J. Davis et al. (2011), "The Angular Diameter and Fundamental Parameters of Sirius A", PASA 28, 58 (2011PASA...28...58D).
- **Vega**: equatorial radius 2.726 ± 0.006 Suns, polar radius 2.418, apparent temperature 9,660 K and rotation period 0.71 days, J. D. Monnier et al. (2012), "Resolving Vega and the inclination controversy with CHARA/MIRC", ApJ 761, L3 (2012ApJ...761L...3M).
- **Arcturus**: 25.4 ± 0.2 Suns and 4,286 K, I. Ramírez and C. Allende Prieto (2011), "Fundamental Parameters and Chemical Composition of Arcturus", ApJ 743, 135 (2011ApJ...743..135R), from the weighted mean of the interferometric angular diameters, 21.06 ± 0.17 mas.
- **Aldebaran**: 44.2 ± 0.9 Suns, a limb-darkened diameter of 20.58 ± 0.03 mas, and 3,934 K, A. Richichi and V. Roccatagliata (2005), "Aldebaran's angular diameter: how well do we know it?", A&A 433, 305 (2005A&A...433..305R).
- **Rigel**: 78.9 ± 7.4 Suns from the limb-darkened diameter 2.75 ± 0.01 mas (Aufdenberg et al. 2008) and 12,100 K (Przybilla et al. 2010), as listed by E. Moravveji et al. (2012), "Asteroseismology of the Nearby SN-II Progenitor: Rigel Part I", ApJ 747, 108 (2012ApJ...747..108M).
- **Antares**: 680 Suns from the limb-darkened diameter 37.38 ± 0.06 mas, and 3,660 K, K. Ohnaka et al. (2013), "High spectral resolution imaging of the dynamical atmosphere of the red supergiant Antares in the CO first overtone lines with VLTI/AMBER", A&A 555, A24 (2013A&A...555A..24O). The error (-99, +139) is worked out here from the 17 percent error of the parallax the paper uses.
- **Betelgeuse**: the limb-darkened diameter 44.28 ± 0.15 mas, X. Haubois et al. (2009), "Imaging the spotty surface of Betelgeuse in the H band", A&A 508, 923 (2009A&A...508..923H), and the distance 222 (+48, -34) pc, G. M. Harper et al. (2017), "An Updated 2017 Astrometric Solution for Betelgeuse", AJ 154, 11 (2017AJ....154...11H); the radius, 1,057 (+228, -162) Suns, is half the angle times the distance, worked out here. 3,650 K: E. M. Levesque et al. (2005), "The Effective Temperature Scale of Galactic Red Supergiants: Cool, But Not As Cool As We Thought", ApJ 628, 973 (2005ApJ...628..973L). Not used: the 764 Suns of M. Joyce et al. (2020), ApJ 902, 63 (2020ApJ...902...63J), which come from stellar models.
- **VY Canis Majoris**: 1,420 ± 120 Suns from the Rosseland angular diameter 11.3 ± 0.3 mas at 1.17 ± 0.08 kpc, and 3,490 K, M. Wittkowski et al. (2012), "Fundamental properties and atmospheric structure of the red supergiant VY CMa based on VLTI/AMBER spectro-interferometry", A&A 540, L12 (2012A&A...540L..12W); the distance is the mean of the radio parallaxes of Choi et al. (2008) and B. Zhang et al. (2012), ApJ 744, 23 (2012ApJ...744...23Z).
- **WOH G64**: 1,540 Suns (an error of 5 percent) and 3,400 ± 25 K, from the spectrum and the brightness at the Large Magellanic Cloud's 50 kpc, E. M. Levesque et al. (2009), "The Physical Properties of the Red Supergiant WOH G64: The Largest Star Known?", AJ 137, 4744 (2009AJ....137.4744L). No angular diameter of the star itself has been measured. Named and not used: 1,730 Suns from a dust model, K. Ohnaka et al. (2008), A&A 484, 371 (2008A&A...484..371O); the star changed between 2009 and 2016, K. Ohnaka et al. (2024), A&A 691, L15 (2024A&A...691L..15O).
- **UY Scuti**: 1,708 ± 192 Suns from the Rosseland angular diameter 5.48 ± 0.10 mas and 3,365 K, B. Arroyo-Torres et al. (2013), "The atmospheric structure and fundamental parameters of the red supergiants AH Sco, UY Sct and KW Sgr", A&A 554, A76 (2013A&A...554A..76A). The distance of about 2.9 kpc is an old estimate and not a parallax, so the size is marked estimated. Named and not used: 909 Suns, from photometry and a Gaia distance, S. Healy et al. (2024), "Red Supergiant Candidates for Multimessenger Monitoring of the Next Galactic Supernova", MNRAS 529, 3630 (Gaia's value is not copied).
- **Stephenson 2-18**: 3,200 K and log L = 5.64 (at 5.5 kpc) from a dust-shell fit, T. K. T. Fok et al. (2012), "Maser Observations of Westerlund 1 and Comprehensive Considerations on Maser Properties of Red Supergiants Associated with Massive Clusters", ApJ 760, 65 (2012ApJ...760...65F); the distance is the cluster's kinematic one, S. Deguchi et al. (2010), PASJ 62, 391 (2010PASJ...62..391D), with the range 5.83 (+1.91, -0.78) kpc of B. Davies et al. (2007), ApJ 671, 781 (2007ApJ...671..781D). The radius, 2,150 (+710, -290) Suns, is worked out here by the Stefan-Boltzmann law (the error is the distance's alone). No angular diameter has been measured.
- **The Earth and Jupiter** beside them are the package's own facts: equatorial radii 6,378.137 km and 71,492 km, IAU and NASA (above).

The star's look (the cells, the spots, the glow) is invented to look right and says so; only the temperature, the radius and the class are the papers'.

## Surface pictures

The painted looks' pictures in `textures/` (the 512 and 1024 pixel widths are also in the package's `dist/textures/`) are made by `scripts/make-textures.py` from public-domain imagery on Wikimedia Commons. The script reads each file's licence from Commons' own metadata and stops unless it is public domain or CC0; the originals are downloaded to `.cache/` and never kept in the repository. `textures/SOURCES.json` records the file pages, licences and credits as read on 2026-11-02.

## Finer map tiles

`scripts/make-body-tiles.py` cuts the big public-domain maps of 15 bodies into a pyramid of 512 pixel WebP tiles (docs/guides/close-zoom.md): 3552 tiles and 76.5 MB in all. The tiles are built in CI and served by GitHub Pages beside the demo at `/tiles/`; they are not in git and not in the npm package. Each source is downloaded once, and a file whose SHA-256 is not the one below is never used. The licence of each source on Wikimedia Commons was read from Commons' own metadata on 2026-11-09 and is public domain for every one of them; they are the files the 2048 pictures above were made from (`scripts/make-textures.py` reads the same metadata and stops unless it says public domain), taken here at their full size. The two NASA sources are not on Commons: their pages are named below.

NASA's terms, read on 2026-11-09 at https://www.nasa.gov/nasa-brand-center/images-and-media/ (NASA Images and Media Usage Guidelines): "NASA content – images, audio, video, and media files used in the rendition of 3-dimensional models, such as texture maps and polygon data in any format – generally are not subject to copyright in the United States." and "NASA should be acknowledged as the source of the material." USGS and JPL maps are works of the United States government. Not endorsed by NASA, the USGS or any mission; no NASA insignia or logotype is used.

A tile is the package's own 2048 picture of the body, enlarged, with the source's detail added where the source really holds data. So its colour is the 2048 picture's, and the credits of that picture (the table above) apply to the tile as well as the credits of the source below.

## The font of a name on a body

A body's name written on its globe (`body-label`) is drawn in Michroma, by Vernon Adams, "Copyright 2011 The Michroma Project Authors (https://github.com/googlefonts/Michroma-font)", under the SIL Open Font License, Version 1.1, with no Reserved Font Name. The licence was read on 2026-11-11: it allows the font to be used, bundled, embedded and redistributed with software, as long as the copyright notice and the licence go with it and the font is not sold by itself. This package bundles no font: the file ships unchanged in `@johnmorrisdotca/kazoku-ui` (`dist/body-label/fonts/Michroma-Regular.ttf`, with `OFL.txt` beside it) and is fetched from there only when a page writes a name in it. A page that names its own font fetches nothing.

## The orbiters of the Moon and Mars

`/orbiters` places thirteen spacecraft round the Moon and Mars from mean orbital elements with secular rates fitted by `scripts/make-orbiter-data.py` to state vectors from NASA/JPL's Horizons system (https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/api/horizons.api), fetched 2026-11-11, one request per craft, with the fit's error against Horizons stated in the data. Horizons' API page states no terms of use, rate limits or citation rule (read 2026-11-11); its data are NASA's and in the public domain, credited "NASA/JPL-Caltech". The facts about each craft (operator, launch, instruments, mission state) are typed from the same craft's Horizons object page, read 2026-11-11, in our own words; the Japanese names and lines are ours. Nothing is taken from any other site. Craft whose track Horizons no longer carries (MAVEN, CAPSTONE) and Tianwen-1, whose only record is a volunteers' cruise track, are marked modelled or without a path, never shown as live.
